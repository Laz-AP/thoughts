To understand the story of architecture is to understand that it is a story of innovation. Throughout human history, we have continuously worked to improve the places where we live and work. Our access to technology, tools, and better materials has allowed architects and engineers to push the boundaries of what is feasible, while contractors and tradespeople provide the practical feedback that continues the innovation loop. Architecture is a story of innovation because buildings bring together people from many walks of life. Many of the major innovations in the AEC industry have been tools for improving communication among all parties.

Chicago Innovate has continued this legacy of communication, serving as a pillar of collaboration in Chicago for the past five years. Organizers Hiram Rodriguez, Anthony Viola, Moses Scott, and Katy Liang have created common ground where people across the AEC industry can gather, present their work, have meaningful conversations, and push the industry forward. This year's program was a four-day event. Day 1 consisted of masterclasses on Thursday, September 24, at the beautiful Atrium Club on Level 7. Friday, September 25, was Day 2: the symposium, held at 800 Fulton Market on the second floor of the impressive lobby. The weekend concluded with a hackathon at Gensler's office. Overall, the conference showed that innovation begins with human-centered communication and problem-solving.

See the event webpage [here](https://www.chicagoinnovate.tech/).

## Panel 1 – Tools and Technology

The first panel of the symposium, "Tools + Technology," was moderated by [John McGarry](https://www.linkedin.com/in/john-mcgarry-aia-ncarb-02601438/) of Gensler and featured [Danny Karas](https://www.linkedin.com/in/danny-karas/) of Thornton Tomasetti (TT), [Francisco Maranchello](https://www.linkedin.com/in/franmaranchello/) of Conduit, and [Thomas Schneider](https://www.linkedin.com/in/thomas-schneider-b0210057/) and [Ryan Kukuraitis](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ryan-kukuraitis-10649831/) of Adrian Smith + Gordon Gill Architecture (ASGG). Each panelist discussed how their firm is approaching the creation and use of innovative tools and technologies to accomplish tasks that were previously impossible or impractical.

John opened the day with a presentation on how computational design has taken root in Detroit through the co.de.D group, as well as how AIA Detroit created an award for Computational Design Excellence. Danny then showed how CORE Studio creates dozens of tools that can be used across TT. CORE Studio has been developing tools to facilitate VDC, or virtual design and construction, including through the use of 4D modeling. This has been critical for communicating real-world constraints to clients and demonstrating that existing facilities can remain operational during construction.

Francisco Maranchello of Conduit spoke about bridging the knowledge gap between designers and manufacturers. He described custom tools developed at Conduit to accelerate the coordination of designs with available manufacturing stock and capacity. Finally, Thomas Schneider and Ryan Kukuraitis presented ASGG's approach that "any tool is viable if it helps get the project done." The studio's lean staffing and the enormous scale of its projects require team members to work as efficiently as possible. They shared how their process has evolved and how complex workflows can be coordinated across a team.

The overarching themes of the panel included avoiding black boxes; treating scripts as "players," rather than code that project teams must decipher; recognizing that "close enough" can be powerful; and prioritizing modularity in any new workflow, especially those involving AI.

## Panel 2 – Advanced Materials and Construction

The second panel, "Advanced Materials + Construction," was moderated by Mario Romero, Perkins&Will's senior digital practice manager. His brief introduction to the panelists emphasized the importance of connecting our work to users and grounding our processes in human-centered principles.

[Dr. Tiffany Cheng](https://www.linkedin.com/in/tiffknee/), an assistant professor at Cornell University, presented her research on bio-based materials and bio-inspired structures. She explained how her work intersects with multiple areas of expertise, including biology, structural engineering, and robotics. She showcased a current project that uses a passive, removable exterior shading structure inspired by pinecones. Installed nearly three years ago, the structure was still functioning. The project faced many of the challenges typical of an architecture project, including negotiating with building officials and coordinating with contractors.

[Josh Dortzbach](https://www.linkedin.com/in/josh-dortzbach-se-pe-0340985/), a founding partner at Forefront, shared his journey from growing up in an African country to eventually moving to the United States. He explained how working as a carpenter to pay for school taught him a valuable lesson: understanding and communicating with contractors and tradespeople provides a significant opportunity for architects and engineers. He emphasized that good design and successful projects consider the whole picture and incorporate input from as many people as possible.

[Sherman Ikemoto](https://www.linkedin.com/in/shermanikemoto/) of Cadence then shared how digital twins are having a significant impact on data centers. Cadence works with partners such as NVIDIA to fully model and synchronize their data centers, or "AI factories," allowing teams to run sophisticated analyses before committing to upgrades or construction. These digital twins allow complex decisions to be tracked and made with greater confidence.

The panel's main themes included moving from digital to physical and back to digital in the development of new materials and construction applications; understanding the limitations of models; learning how to communicate with people so they feel comfortable with innovation; and addressing educational gaps among newer hires. Josh closed by answering the question, "When should tradespeople be involved in the process?" His answer was immediate: "Immediately."

## Panel 3 – Innovation in Practice

Following an after-lunch sponsor talk by [Nick Cameron](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ncameron1/), Perkins&Will Digital Practice's fearless leader, the third panel, "Innovation in Practice," began. [Kevin Kosciulek](https://www.linkedin.com/in/kevin-kosciulek-aia-958445ba/), director of technology practice at bKL Architecture and co-chair of AIA Chicago's TAP, explained that the topic was his favorite because lessons learned emerge where boots-on-the-ground practice meets innovative technology.

[Madelaine Murray](https://www.linkedin.com/in/madelaine-murray/), an architect at Perkins&Will, and I began the panel by sharing lessons about how project teams with integrated digital practice can quickly adapt to complex project changes. We shared lessons from a recent large healthcare project in Chicago and described advanced Revit workflows that addressed multiple constraints, including rapid calculation of window-to-wall ratios, streamlined documentation of bird-protection measures, high rendering fidelity, and a lightweight, simple shell model.

We also shared how an exercise in editing and simplification can bring greater clarity to a project's design. [Chris O'Hara](https://www.linkedin.com/in/studionyl/), founding partner of Studio NYL, presented the firm's incredible work from around the world and explained why conventional tools are insufficient for the complexity of its project types. Studio NYL's use of computational design, along with its efforts to bridge the gap between design and construction, creates real value when delivering work. This presentation echoed Josh Dortzbach's earlier comments about the importance of connecting design to construction.

The final presentation came from [Yiliang Shao](https://www.linkedin.com/in/yiliang-shao-ab01b7227/), senior computational design specialist at SOM. Yiliang explained how the steps of the design process have gradually been streamlined or eliminated by increasingly sophisticated tools. Similar to the modularization of workflows discussed during the first panel, Yiliang showed how, when AI is introduced, the remaining human role is to make the decisions that move the process forward.

Key takeaways from the panel included the limitations of flattened 2D deliverables, which create challenges for innovation; the importance of communicating with project teams and developing processes with them to encourage ownership and buy-in; and the understanding that AI is a tool in our kit but cannot be trusted to make decisions. The panel also raised an important question: If AI automates tasks we once performed, how will we know which decisions are right, and what should we teach the next generation?

## Panel 4 – Future Technologies

The final panel, "Future Technologies," capped the day with a forward-looking discussion of the AEC industry. [Sonja Flint](https://www.linkedin.com/in/sonjaneva/), an architect and member of bKL's technology practice, moderated the conversation and introduced the panelists.

Dr. [Julide Bozoglu](https://www.linkedin.com/in/julidebozoglu/), director of technology at Goettsch Partners, began with insights into how AI will affect the processes and work we deliver on our projects. She shared an example of how she developed custom Revit plug-ins in a relatively short period of time using AI, despite having little prior coding experience. This allowed her and her team to focus on higher-impact project tasks.

[Thomas Kearns](https://www.linkedin.com/in/thomasmartinkearns/), digital innovation strategist at Perkins&Will, followed by sharing how his experiences in the AEC and gaming industries have informed his view of emerging technologies. Geospatial models may eventually contain the project information needed to complete the work, leveraging AI and other advanced technologies to augment our abilities and produce better results more efficiently. However, there is also a danger that this shift could displace many professionals as fewer people become capable of doing more.

[Nitsa Bartov](https://www.linkedin.com/in/nitsan-bartov/), a PhD fellow at Henning Larsen and the Royal Danish Academy, gave the final presentation of the day. Their fascinating lecture focused on how their work and firm are taking steps to better educate staff about AI and how it works. Bartov emphasized that better education allows staff to understand conceptually how to use these tools on their projects.

Bartov also explained the real-world impacts AI is already having on its users, including research suggesting that, within a few weeks, people may experience a decline in their ability to perform tasks that AI has taken over. Bartov encouraged the audience to identify the aspects that define architecture's value, understand how AI will affect those areas, and focus mentorship and education on increasing students' ability to contribute to them.

The key takeaways from the panel were that AI will still require human judgment; that learning from the past and developing critical thinking and a strong design eye will provide an advantage over AI; and that even AI's mistakes can be embraced as opportunities for creativity.

## Key Takeaways

Chicago Innovate lived up to its promise to bring together a diverse group of individuals to share their expertise and insights with the wider Chicago AEC community. One key takeaway that emerged repeatedly was that communication sits at the center of innovation. People are best able to innovate and find solutions when working with others, especially those whose experiences and values differ from their own.

Furthermore, as professionals, we have an obligation to better prepare the next generation of practitioners who will work with increasingly sophisticated technologies. For the AEC industry to succeed, we need to focus on where we provide value as individuals and use AI to augment our ability to produce high-quality work.
